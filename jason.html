The Secret Paths of City Animals

A city may seem an unlikely home for wild animals. Streets are busy, buildings are tall, and bright lights can make night seem almost like day. Yet many animals have found ways to live alongside people. Some even use parts of the city that humans barely notice.

At dusk, a small possum leaves a tree beside a school playground. It needs to reach another patch of trees to find food. The shortest route crosses a wide road. 20 ______ Instead, it travels along a row of garden fences until it reaches a narrow strip of bushland.

This strip is called a wildlife corridor. It connects places where animals can shelter and feed. 21 ______ Without such connections, animals may be trapped in small patches of habitat, even when another suitable patch is close by.

Not every corridor looks like a forest. A line of street trees, a creek bank or even a series of back gardens can provide a route. 22 ______ A bird might fly between trees, while a lizard may need continuous ground cover to avoid being seen by predators.

Wildlife researchers sometimes place cameras near these routes. The cameras take photographs when movement triggers a sensor. 23 ______ They can then discover which animals use a route and whether they travel mainly during the day or at night.

But a corridor is not automatically safe. Domestic cats may wait near it, and strong lights can disturb animals that normally move in darkness. 24 ______ For example, planting thick shrubs may offer shelter, while directing lights away from the vegetation can keep the route darker.

A few residents have begun leaving small gaps beneath their fences so ground-dwelling animals can pass. Others have replaced bare lawns with native plants. 25 ______ Taken together, these modest changes may turn isolated gardens into a connected network.

Sentence choices (A–G)

A Different species, however, need different kinds of cover to move safely.

B For this reason, planners must think about more than simply joining two green spaces.

C These images allow scientists to study visitors without following them through the bushes.

D Such small actions may appear insignificant when considered one at a time.

E Crossing it would be risky, especially with cars moving quickly in both directions.

F In other words, the corridor acts as a bridge between separate habitats.

G Most animals therefore prefer to remain inside large buildings during daylight.

Question 26 · Learning Something New

Question 26 Read the four short extracts about learning something new. Which extract suggests that advice which seemed unhelpful at first later proved valuable?

Extract A: I had never used a telescope before. The first time I looked through it, the moon seemed so close that I forgot how cold the night was.

Extract B: My swimming coach kept telling me to slow down. I thought that was strange advice for a race. Weeks later, I realised that a calmer start left me enough energy to finish strongly.

Extract C: At the pottery class, everyone else shaped their bowls quickly. Mine leaned to one side, and I wished I had practised at home.

Extract D: Learning chess was exciting because every game introduced a new problem. Even losing made me curious about what I might try next.
