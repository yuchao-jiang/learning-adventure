The Secret Paths of City Animals

A city may seem an unlikely home for wild animals. Streets are busy, buildings are tall, and bright lights can make night seem almost like day. Yet many animals have found ways to live alongside people. Some even use parts of the city that humans barely notice.

At dusk, a small possum leaves a tree beside a school playground. It needs to reach another patch of trees to find food. The shortest route crosses a wide road. Instead, it travels along a row of garden fences until it reaches a narrow strip of bushland.

This strip is called a wildlife corridor. It connects places where animals can shelter and feed. Without such connections, animals may be trapped in small patches of habitat, even when another suitable patch is close by.

Not every corridor looks like a forest. A line of street trees, a creek bank or even a series of back gardens can provide a route. A bird might fly between trees, while a lizard may need continuous ground cover to avoid being seen by predators.

Wildlife researchers sometimes place cameras near these routes. The cameras take photographs when movement triggers a sensor. They can then discover which animals use a route and whether they travel mainly during the day or at night.

But a corridor is not automatically safe. Domestic cats may wait near it, and strong lights can disturb animals that normally move in darkness. For example, planting thick shrubs may offer shelter, while directing lights away from the vegetation can keep the route darker.

A few residents have begun leaving small gaps beneath their fences so ground-dwelling animals can pass. Others have replaced bare lawns with native plants. Taken together, these modest changes may turn isolated gardens into a connected network.

Four matching texts

Read each writer's experience, then answer Questions 26–33 below.

Text A — Mira — The Midnight Garden

I used to think the garden behind our apartment was lifeless after sunset. One evening, a neighbour invited me to help count moths. We waited beside a lamp, noting the different patterns on their wings. At first I was disappointed: I had expected something dramatic. But as I learned to distinguish one moth from another, I began to notice how busy the garden really was. Now I leave the outdoor light off when it is not needed, so the insects are less disturbed.

Text B — Oliver — The Repair Table

When the handle snapped off my favourite mug, I was ready to throw it away. At the community repair table, a volunteer showed me how to mend it safely for display, though it could no longer be used for drinking. The repair was far from invisible. In fact, the join made a crooked line across the mug. I had wanted it to look new, but the volunteer said the mark could be a reminder of the work that saved it. I still keep the mug on my shelf.

Text C — Leila — The Borrowed Map

During our class walk, I was given a map and asked to guide my group to a lookout. I hurried ahead, certain that I knew the way. When we reached a locked gate, I insisted the map was wrong. Only after a classmate pointed out a tiny symbol did I realise I had mistaken a walking track for a service road. We retraced our steps. I felt embarrassed, but on the way back I began asking the others what they noticed before making a decision.

Text D — Theo — The Quiet Stage

I agreed to read a poem at the school assembly, then spent a week wishing I had refused. Practising alone was easy; standing in front of people was not. On the day, my voice shook through the opening lines. I spotted my teacher in the audience, slowly nodding to the rhythm. I concentrated on the next line instead of the whole crowd. I did not suddenly become fearless, but I finished without rushing. Afterwards, I volunteered to read again at a smaller event.
